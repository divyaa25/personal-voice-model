I'll start by saying that my understanding of strategy used to be pretty narrow. It was all about analyzing your own business, mapping out the competition, and optimizing what you already had. I thought of a strategist as someone who focused on their own thing, making it better, faster, and more efficient. It was a very inward-looking approach, and I didn't think of it as something you could apply to other areas of life.

My background is in data science and engineering, where strategy is often about picking the right model, optimizing a pipeline, or making a system more efficient. I had a pretty technical and bounded view of strategic thinking, defined by the problem in front of me. I didn't think of it as creative, and I certainly didn't think of it as something you could borrow from other fields or domains. It was all about solving the problem, not about looking at the bigger picture.

But then I learned about the 4 C's framework, and it completely changed my perspective. Strategy is no longer just about optimizing what you have; it's about engaging with the entire landscape around you. Contrast taught me to question assumptions, to look at things from a different angle. Combination showed me that the most interesting value often comes from bringing together things that don't normally go together. Constraint revealed that limitations can actually be inputs to strategy, forcing you to think creatively. And Context was the biggest shift of all: the realization that the best ideas often come from outside your domain, waiting to be noticed and imported.

What surprised me most was how naturally this applies beyond business. I move between technical work and managerial thinking, between engineering and policy, between quantitative analysis and human storytelling. I used to see these as separate modes, but now I see them as a portfolio of contexts I can draw on, sources of analogy, recombination, and contrast that most people in any single field don't have access to. My position at the edges of multiple domains, which I once thought of as a lack of specialization, is actually a strategic asset.

So, how do I plan to put this into practice? Well, the most immediate change is in how I start projects. Before, I would begin by scoping out the technical problem, looking at the data, the models, the outputs. Now, I want to start one step earlier, by asking whether the problem itself is the right one, and whether the approach everyone else is using is worth questioning. That's a small shift, but it's a significant one in terms of outcomes.

More concretely, I'm completing an MS in Computer Engineering, and I'll be heading into roles where technical and business thinking will need to coexist. That boundary is exactly where the 4 C's are most useful, not as a checklist, but as a habit of looking sideways before looking forward. In my prior work in data science, including building a paywall optimization model at a think-tank, the technical problem was never just technical; it was always embedded in questions about user behavior, business value, and organizational priorities. The 4 C's give me a language for navigating that complexity more deliberately.

As for refining this, I don't think the 4 C's framework gets better by studying it more; it gets better by using it, messing it up a few times, and noticing where it breaks down. I expect to reach for combination when contrast is actually needed, or to mistake surface similarity for genuine analogy. Those failures are useful, because they help me build the habit of noticing when I've defaulted to a conventional frame and pausing long enough to ask whether a different C might open something up.

Practically, I plan to keep expanding what Austin Kleon calls idea income, reading across fields I don't work in, paying attention to how problems get solved in fashion, policy, biology, and asking what each one might offer to the technical and managerial problems I actually face. The most useful version of this course, for me, is not the framework itself, but the permission to take seriously the idea that a good solution might come from somewhere completely unexpected. It's the freedom to look sideways, to combine, to contrast, and to find new contexts, that's going to make all the difference.
