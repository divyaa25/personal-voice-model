I. What I Thought Strategy Was
Before this course, my understanding of strategy was narrow and inward-looking. I thought of a strategist primarily as someone who analyzed their own business, someone who mapped competitive landscapes, identified strengths and weaknesses, and optimized what was already there. Strategy, in my mind, was mostly about doing your own thing better. It belonged to business plans and boardrooms. It was something you applied to your organization, not a way of thinking you could bring to everything else.
My background is in data science and engineering, where strategy tends to mean something very specific: picking the right model, optimizing a pipeline, making a system more efficient. I had absorbed a version of strategic thinking that was largely technical and bounded, defined by the edges of the problem in front of me. I did not think of it as creative, and I certainly did not think of it as something that could be borrowed, recombined, or inspired by the world outside the immediate domain.
II. What I Think Strategy Is Now
The 4 C's framework fundamentally changed how I see this. Strategy is no longer something I apply only to the business or project that’s directly in front of me but I see it as a way of engaging with the entire landscape around it. Contrast taught me to question the assumptions that everyone else is treating as fixed. Combination showed me that the most interesting value is often created not by building something new from scratch, but by bringing together things that have never been in the same room before. Constraint revealed that limitations are not obstacles to strategy but inputs to it, the conditions that force genuine creativity. And Context was perhaps the most expansive shift of all: the realization that the best ideas for any domain often live somewhere else entirely, waiting to be noticed and imported.
What surprised me most was how naturally this applies beyond business. I am someone who moves between technical work and managerial thinking, between engineering and policy, between quantitative analysis and human storytelling. I used to see these as separate modes. Now I see them as a portfolio of contexts I can draw on - sources of analogy, recombination, and contrast that most people in any single field simply don't have access to. My position at the edges of multiple domains, which I once thought of as a lack of specialization, is actually a strategic asset in the 4 C's sense.
III. How I Plan to Put This Into Practice
The most immediate change is in how I start projects. Before, I would begin by scoping the technical problem - what data do I have, what model fits, what does the output need to look like. Now I want to begin one step earlier, by asking whether the problem itself is the right one, and whether the approach everyone else is using is worth questioning. That is a small shift in sequence but a significant one in outcomes.
More concretely, I am completing an MS in Computer Engineering, heading into roles where technical and business thinking will need to coexist. That boundary is exactly where the 4 C's are most useful - not as a checklist, but as a habit of looking sideways before looking forward. In my prior work in data science including building a paywall optimization model at a think-tank, the technical problem was never just technical. It was always embedded in questions about user behavior, business value, and organizational priorities. The 4 C's give me a language for navigating that complexity more deliberately.
IV. How I Plan to Keep Refining This
I do not think the 4 C's framework gets refined by studying it more. It gets refined by using it badly a few times and noticing where it broke down. I expect to reach for combination when contrast was actually needed, or to mistake surface similarity for genuine analogy. Those failures are useful. What I want to build is the habit of noticing when I have defaulted to a conventional frame and pausing long enough to ask whether a different C might open something up.
Practically, I plan to keep expanding what Kleon calls idea income - reading across fields I do not work in, paying attention to how problems get solved in fashion, in policy, in biology and asking what each one might offer to the technical and managerial problems I actually face. The most useful version of this course, for me, is not the framework itself. It is the permission to take seriously the idea that a good solution might come from somewhere completely unexpected.
